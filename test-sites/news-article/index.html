The last ferry to Skerrow

For ninety years a single boat has kept a community of two hundred people connected to the mainland. Its retirement forces a question nobody on the island wants to answer.

By Marguerite Alderley · Photographs by Tobias Wren · 14 March 2025 · 12 minute read

The Morag Ellis leaves Penhallow Quay at 7.40 every morning, give or take the tide, and for most of the winter she carries more diesel drums than people. On the Tuesday I joined her there were nine passengers, a pallet of tinned peaches, two crates of hens and a retired lighthouse keeper who insisted, with some force, that the crossing had been better in the old days, when the swell was worse. Nobody argued with him. On Skerrow, arguing about the past is a leisure activity, and one is expected to pace oneself.

The council's report, published in January, describes the vessel as "approaching the end of her economic life, with maintenance costs that now exceed the revenue she generates by a factor of three, and a hull survey that raises concerns we cannot responsibly ignore".1 It is a careful sentence, written by careful people, and it contains the word "responsibly" in a place where a less careful writer might have put "regrettably". Islanders noticed. At the public meeting in the village hall, the phrase was read out twice, once by a councillor and once, rather more pointedly, by the woman who runs the shop.

"You cannot replace a ferry with a spreadsheet. A ferry is how my mother got to hospital." Ishbel Carrick, shopkeeper, Skerrow

The numbers are not in dispute. Skerrow has 212 permanent residents, down from 340 in 1981, and an age profile that the report politely calls "mature". The crossing takes fifty-five minutes in fair weather and, on the bad days, as long as the captain judges prudent, which is sometimes never. In 2024 the service was cancelled on forty-one days. The council estimates that a replacement vessel of similar capacity would cost £4.2 million, a figure that rises to nearly six if the quay at the island end has to be rebuilt to take a deeper draught.

Where the money goes

Nobody I spoke to believed the council was acting in bad faith. Most of them, in fact, went out of their way to say so. What they objected to was the shape of the conversation: a set of options drawn up on the mainland, each with a price attached, and each described in the language of efficiency and resilience that, to island ears, tends to precede a reduction in service. The three options on the table are a new boat, a smaller boat with a reduced timetable, and a subsidised water-taxi that would run on demand.

Take the water-taxi. Its supporters, led by a younger group of incomers who work remotely, point out that it would run seven days a week, including the days when the big boat is laid up, and that a fast catamaran is less exposed to the swell at the harbour mouth. Its opponents, who are mostly older and mostly born on the island, point out that a catamaran cannot carry a coffin, a cow, or a fridge-freezer, and that all three have, in living memory, needed to make the crossing at short notice.

There is a deeper disagreement underneath the practical one. The ferry is not just a vehicle. For the islanders it is a kind of public room, the one place where everybody from the schoolchildren to the district nurse is obliged to sit together for the better part of an hour. News travels on it. Marriages, one woman told me, have been quietly arranged on it. "A water-taxi carries six people who don't know each other," she said. "The ferry carries a village."

If the boat goes, the island does not end in a day. It ends in a decade, politely, one empty house at a time. Dr Callum Hesketh, island GP

What the weather decides

The sea, as ever, has opinions. The marine engineer who surveyed the hull, a soft-spoken man named Piers Dunmore, told me that the Morag Ellis was "structurally sound in the way that an old oak is structurally sound": capable of standing for a long time, provided nobody asks it to do anything new. The survey found corrosion in two ballast tanks and a crack in the rudder stock that had been repaired, he said, "with more love than welding". None of this makes her unsafe today. All of it makes her expensive tomorrow.

By the time we reached the island the morning had cleared to a hard, bright blue, and the lighthouse keeper had fallen asleep against a coil of rope with his cap over his face. A boy of about eight, who had spent the crossing counting the gulls, announced that there had been forty-seven, and then, with the flat certainty of the young, that the ferry would obviously not be taken away because "it's the ferry". The shopkeeper, standing at the rail with a crate of eggs on her hip, said nothing. She looked at the quay, at the hens, at the boy, and then at the water, and I could not tell whether she was agreeing with him or grieving in advance.

The council will decide in June. Until then the Morag Ellis will keep leaving Penhallow Quay at 7.40, give or take the tide, carrying peaches and hens and the quiet, stubborn business of a place that has no intention of being a statistic.
